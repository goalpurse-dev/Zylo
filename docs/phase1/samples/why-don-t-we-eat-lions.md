## Full narration script (plain text, exactly as it would be sent to TTS)

You’re staring at a mounted lion head, a small paper price tag hanging from a mounted lion head, while someone across the yard flips a perfectly normal steak on a grill. The scene feels wrong — like two different value systems in one frame: trophy versus dinner. It matters because lions are not common: the IUCN assessment in 2016 estimates about twenty three thousand to thirty nine thousand wild African lions globally, and the report points to habitat loss, prey declines and direct killing as the main threats to their survival. Why don't people usually eat lions? The short answer I’ll build is: there isn’t one reason — several forces stack together. First: ecology. Apex predators like lions tend to occur at much lower densities and reproduce more slowly than typical prey animals — that’s ecological basic math (inferred from the IUCN 2016 context). In plain terms: there are far fewer lions per square kilometer than antelope or bushbuck, so lions are a scarce, unreliable source of meat compared with common bushmeat species. Second: the law. The IUCN 2016 assessment highlights human killing and unsustainable exploitation as key threats and points to conservation measures and protected‑area rules as the response. That implies there are legal and enforcement barriers — in other words, killing and selling a lion carries higher legal risk than selling ordinary bushmeat, so the legal supply and commercial trade are reduced. Third: economics. The IUCN 2016 context emphasizes non‑consumptive values like tourism and the role of lions in ecosystems, and from that it’s a reasonable inference that in many places a live lion or a trophy often brings more money than one one‑time set of meat cuts (IUCN 2016, inference). So that visible price tag can be shorthand: the lion is often worth more alive, as a tourist draw or as a trophy, than as a piece of meat. Fourth: culture. A search of the IUCN 2016 assessment does not turn up authoritative ethnographic records showing routine, widespread consumption of lion meat across Africa (IUCN 2016). That absence in the conservation record suggests lions are often wrapped in social meanings — prestige, taboo, or special status — that make them unsuitable as everyday food in many places. Fifth: practical and health concerns. The IUCN 2016 assessment does not provide numbers on pathogen prevalence, edible meat yield per carcass, or human mortality associated with hunting lions (IUCN 2016), so those exact practical and health details require other studies. Practically speaking, big predators are harder and more dangerous to kill and process than antelope, and that raises the personal risk and expense for anyone trying to make meat from a lion into a regular food source. But here’s the twist: none of this is absolute. The IUCN 2016 assessment does not give a continent‑wide annual total for lions killed for bushmeat or non‑trophy causes (IUCN 2016), and absence from that assessment doesn’t prove lions were never eaten locally. In short: the stacked reasons usually make lions poor regular food, but in rare, specific circumstances — local scarcity of other protein, weak enforcement, or particular cultural practices — the calculus can flip. Now go back to that image: a small paper price tag hanging from a mounted lion head. That tag is rarely a literal meat price. Instead it often stands for a stack of other values and barriers: tourism income and trophy markets, legal protections that raise risk, cultural status, and the ecological rarity and danger of killing a lion. The IUCN 2016 assessment doesn’t list meat prices or tourism tallies, but it emphasizes those non‑consumptive values and the threats that make lions scarce, which supports the idea that non‑meat value often outweighs meat value. The result: lions aren’t typical food because several reinforcing barriers stack up — ecology, law, money, culture, and practical risk — and usually the stack wins. But sometimes, in narrow local conditions, one or more barriers fall away and the pattern flips.
