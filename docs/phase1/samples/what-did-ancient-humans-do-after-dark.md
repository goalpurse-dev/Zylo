## Full narration script (plain text, exactly as it would be sent to TTS)

You crouch close to a single low hearth in total dark, the coal glow painting faces in orange. A single charred stick leans against the hearth, blackened at one end — possibly a torch. Experimental tests found a simple charred wooden torch produced a visible flame for sixty one minutes in one study. Fire and cooking raised available dietary energy and, Richard Wrangham argues, campfires also extended waking hours to promote social exchange and social learning around the fire. What did ancient humans actually do after sunset, and how do we know? Archaeologists use a multiproxy toolkit that treats charcoal, soot and microcharcoal as direct proxies for Palaeolithic lighting and night activity, and combines sensory, ethnographic and geoarchaeological methods like micromorphology, use‑wear and residue analysis to reconstruct perception, social context and subsistence. Hearths are the base evidence for after‑dark life. At Kebara Cave researchers describe discrete hearth lenses between three and fifteen centimeters thick and commonly twenty to eighty centimeters across, sometimes even thirty centimeters thick and over one hundred centimeters across, which mark repeated controlled fires. Chemical and microscopic studies use charcoal anatomy, phytoliths and pyromarker compounds to tell plant fuel from burned bone or fat, so the fuel signature comes with a biological fingerprint. In Chauvet researchers ran radiocarbon dates on cave floor charcoal and charcoal marks: seventy one dates on floor charcoal and forty two on charcoal markings, which supports the interpretation that people brought and used burning wood in the cave. Settlements like Dolní Věstonice show complex, multi‑phased hearths and dense artifact clusters that archaeologists read as recurrent fireside congregation and activity palimpsests. Ethnography helps fill behavior: Polly Wiessner records that among the Ju slash hoansi over eighty percent of nighttime campfire conversation is elaborate storytelling, a pattern that points to concentrated social life by fire. If you have sat by a small fire you feel the same focus; night shifts talk into narrative and teaching. Some art gives direct clues to deep time lighting. Uranium‑series dating at Sulawesi returns a minimum age of thirty nine thousand nine hundred years before present for a hand stencil and roughly thirty five thousand four hundred years for a nearby animal painting, which shows humans were making low‑relief imagery very early. But cave contexts and pigment smears combine with charcoal smears in caves to suggest the use of torches; the question is whether those visits were at night or simply in deep dark cave zones. Here is the honest complication: engineers of light do not equal night visits. Methodological work warns that deep cave zones are dark at noon too, so evidence of artificial illumination tells us people engineered light, but it does not by itself prove the activity happened after sunset. Regional and temporal variability means some groups probably used light to extend social and production time after dark, and others did not. That single charred stick, blackened at one end, can be tested against experimental torches and residue criteria to argue it was used as portable lighting. Residue and use‑wear specialists recommend optical microscopy of residues, soil micromorphology, chemical characterization and comparison with experimental reference collections as the decision path. If microscopic residues and wear patterns match experimental torch analogues, and controls exclude soil contaminants, then that little stick becomes a strong, reasoned argument for portable light rather than a burned branch left by chance; experimental torches that burn about sixty one minutes give a practical analogue for how long such a light could have worked. So what did people do after dark? They didn't vanish into mystery, and they didn't simply recreate daylight. With fire and simple portable lights they extended and reshaped time into concentrated social evenings, focused production and selective low‑light foraging or guarding, and sometimes deep‑cave art or ritual. The tiny objects we planted earlier — the charred stick, the flakes, the pigment smear — are the clues that let us read that behavior. Night, then, is a structured extension of daytime tasks shaped by light and risk. Night: not empty, another kind of day.
